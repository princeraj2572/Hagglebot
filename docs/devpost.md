## Hagglebot

**Problem.** Getting a fair price means hours of research and awkward haggling with strangers. Most people just pay the sticker price, and sellers know it.

**Solution.** Hagglebot is an AI buyer agent. You enter what you want, your maximum price and how hard it should haggle (patient, balanced or quick). It opens chats with several sellers in parallel, anchors low, concedes in small steps, and closes only when a price fits your budget. Then it creates a PayPal order; you press "Confirm and Pay", approve in PayPal's checkout, and Hagglebot sends the seller a confirmation containing the PayPal payer-id as proof. A paid deal can be fully refunded from the dashboard through the PayPal Payments API, so the whole order lifecycle (create, capture, refund) is covered. A live dashboard shows every thread, the latest offers, and a Pause killswitch.

**One budget for several wants.** A bundle groups wants (say, a whole desk setup) under one total. Hagglebot negotiates each want within what is left of the total, and the payment step refuses anything that would take it over, so the ceiling holds across purchases too.

**You stay in control.** An optional setting makes Hagglebot stop at an in-budget price and ask you to accept or decline before it agrees to anything, and every negotiation can be downloaded as a transcript with its guardrail notes as an audit trail. A savings panel shows what it saved against the first ask and your budget.

**Agentic commerce, done honestly.** Hagglebot always introduces itself as an AI buyer agent and never impersonates you. The code, not the prompt, decides every price, so the budget can't be exceeded. The language model only phrases each move, and its output is checked: wrong prices, budget leaks and claims to be human are rejected. It walks away after repeated refusals, at the message limit, or on scam signals such as hidden fees and off-platform payment. "Shady Sam" tries exactly that and is refused, and "Injection Ivy" tries to talk the agent out of its instructions and is refused too. Every one of these decisions shows up as a **guardrail note** under the message it shaped, so you can see why the agent did what it did, not just that it did it.

**Why it matters.** Haggling is a tax on people who dislike it, and on anyone without the time. An agent that negotiates for you only works if you can trust it with two things: your budget and your money. So the product is not "an AI that talks to sellers", it is the trust layer around it: a hard budget ceiling the model cannot override, a disclosure that it is an AI, scam walk-aways, a full explanation of every decision, and payment through a checkout you approve yourself. As agents start to buy on our behalf, that is the part every one of them will need, and here it is built, tested and visible.

**Where it goes next.** The seller side is a one-method `Seller` interface (`respond(context)`), and today it is backed by simulated personas so the demo is repeatable and safe. A real marketplace would be one more adapter behind that same interface: Hagglebot would read a listing, talk to the seller through the marketplace's own messaging, and the rest (strategy, guardrails, PayPal checkout, refund) stays unchanged. The same engine could also help sellers answer lowball offers consistently. We have built the listings half (Channel3, eBay and Etsy search); contacting real sellers and a live marketplace adapter are not built.

**Beat the bot.** A seller can also be a real person: scan the QR code and haggle against Hagglebot from your phone. A safety scorecard replays 200 negotiations and counts budget breaches, and a haggle copilot drafts your next message for a listing on a real marketplace, which you send yourself.

**Real listings.** "Find products" searches Channel3's product catalog (and eBay and Etsy when their keys are set) for the item and turns the real retailer offers (store, price, condition) into sellers. Each one is played by a simulated persona that opens at the real price; no retailer is contacted. A **Marketplace** page lets you search or browse by category. Real stores do not haggle, so each product can be bought at its listed price (straight to PayPal, still capped by your budget) or negotiated in the simulation, and "find similar" surfaces used and refurbished listings, which are more negotiable. The result is real prices to negotiate against, with the negotiation itself still simulated.

**Tech.** Next.js 15, TypeScript, Tailwind, SQLite, PayPal Orders v2 (official server SDK plus the JS checkout), Channel3 product search, AG Grid for the deal comparison and the marketplace table, and Gemini Flash-Lite with an automatic Groq fallback and a template negotiator if no model is reachable.

**Honest limits.** This is a demo: PayPal sandbox only (no real money), and the sellers (fourteen personas) are simulated, not real people or stores. Real listings come from product search only: no retailer is contacted and nothing is bought from one. The scam detector is heuristic.

**Try it.** Live demo: https://hagglebot-eanj.onrender.com (PayPal sandbox only; the free host sleeps when idle, so the first load can take about a minute). MIT licensed; clone, add sandbox credentials to `.env.local`, and run `pnpm dev`.
